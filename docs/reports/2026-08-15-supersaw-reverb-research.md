# Research pass — reverb on supersaws, Reese basses and saw arps

**Date:** 2026-08-15 · **Organ:** lead · **Requested by:** the human, to inform the
reverb port (`FX-7`) and the coupling-K decision.

Two halves: what producers *say* they like (sentiment, weak evidence, useful for
defaults), and what the algorithm literature says about *why* (stronger, and it
turns out to bear directly on a decision we have open).

---

## Part 1 — Sentiment, by source material

### Supersaw pads and leads: hall and plate, high-cut, pre-delay

The recurring recommendations converge tightly:

- **Hall and plate dominate.** Named repeatedly for "big trance reverb" — hall
  engines for size, plate for density without a long tail.
- **High-cut on the send is near-universal.** *"Bigger, wet reverbs sound great
  on supersaw synths, with high-end cut so it doesn't compete with the brightness
  of the sawtooth waves."* A supersaw is already dense in the top octaves; the
  reverb's job is space, not more brightness.
- **Pre-delay 20–40 ms**, so the dry attack lands before the tail. Decay
  **2–4 s** in a breakdown, **0.8–1.5 s** at a drop.
- **Shimmer is cautioned against** for supersaws specifically — the advice is to
  prefer clean, spacious algorithms that preserve clarity rather than
  pitch-shifted haze on an already-complex source.

### Reese basses: the taboo is real but its reason is obsolete

The most interesting sentiment finding, because the received wisdom and its
justification have come apart:

- The historical rule against reverb on bass was **not about how it sounds** — it
  was a **vinyl-cutting constraint**: reverb is a stereo effect, and stereo
  content in the low end causes phase problems on a cut lathe.
- Modern practice: *"for legato style basses such as a Reese bass, reverb may
  help to create a sense of depth."* The Reese is a **sustained, detuned,
  chorusing** sound — closer to a pad than to a plucked bass — which is exactly
  the material reverb suits.
- Consensus where it survives: keep the **low end mono and dry**, apply space
  above the crossover.

**This is a direct argument for a feature we already ship.** Our bass-mono
crossover (ids 40/41, M/S elliptic) is precisely the mechanism that makes reverb
on a Reese safe. Worth making that pairing explicit in a preset rather than
leaving the user to discover it.

### Sawtooth arps: the signature move is a *ducked* reverb

For arps and plucks the technique named again and again is **sidechaining the
reverb return to the dry signal** — 100% wet send, compressed by the source, so
the tail blooms in the gaps and gets out of the way during notes. *"The reverb
won't muddy up the lead because it ducks out of the way when it's playing."*

Notable for us: this is a **dynamics decision living in the reverb**, not in the
mixer. If our reverb slot ever grows a duck control keyed to the instrument's own
envelope, it would be doing internally what producers currently patch externally.

## Part 2 — The technical half, and it changes a decision we have open

### The Lexicon / Quantec split

There is a genuine, decades-old design schism about whether reverb tails should
be **modulated**:

- **Lexicon** made chorusing *integral to the algorithm* — deliberate pitch
  movement in the tail, used as a **workaround to break up unpleasant
  resonances**. Modulation as medicine.
- **Quantec** deliberately **avoided** it, targeting realism: *no pitch movement
  in the tails*, on the grounds that nature does not chorus a room.

And the mechanism underneath, from the FDN literature: modulation means
time-varying delay lengths, and those micro-variations **break up static
interference patterns that would otherwise produce metallic ringing or tonal
coloration**. The dose-response is well documented — *"small modulation produces
a smooth, animated tail without pitch wobble, while large modulation creates
chorused or dreamy cloud effects"*, and too much *"may create unwanted pitch
modulation."* Co-prime delay lengths are the other standard defence against
coloration.

**Metallic ringing is exactly `FX-7`'s fail criterion.** So the fail mode our ear
test is looking for has a known cause and a known cure, and the cure has a known
cost.

### The finding that matters for HYPERSAW

**Reverb modulation exists to de-correlate a signal that is otherwise too
static. A supersaw is already the least static source there is.**

Our source is a population of detuned oscillators beating against each other —
continuous, dense, pitch-varying de-correlation, generated by the instrument
before the reverb ever sees it. Lexicon-style chorused tails are solving a
problem our source has already solved, and solving it a second time is how you
get a wash.

That yields a testable hypothesis rather than a preference:

> **On a coherent swarm, the reverb should modulate. On a spread swarm, it should
> not.** At high K the population locks toward one phase and the source becomes
> *static* — precisely the condition modulation is medicine for. At low K the
> source is already beating, and adding tail modulation is redundant at best and
> muddy at worst.

**This is an inverse coupling, and it inverts the obvious reading of the
coupling-K question.** The intuitive design — "couple reverb modulation to K so
they move together" — points the wrong way. The defensible version is
**modulation depth ∝ (1 − order parameter)**: the reverb animates exactly when the
swarm stops animating itself.

It is falsifiable and cheap to test in the reverb lab: hold a chord, sweep K from
0 to 1 with reverb modulation fixed, and listen for where the tail starts to
sound metallic. If the metallic onset tracks rising K, the hypothesis holds.

---

## What this changes

1. **`FX-7`'s ear test now has a mechanism attached.** "Metallic ring" is not
   vague — it is under-modulated static interference in the delay network, and
   the fix is a small amount of delay-time modulation, not more diffusion.
2. **The coupling-K decision has a specific proposal to accept or reject**:
   inverse coupling, `mod depth ∝ (1 − R)`, rather than direct. Stated as a
   hypothesis, not a finding — we have not measured it.
3. **Defaults have evidence**: pre-delay 20–40 ms, high-cut on the reverb path,
   decay in the 0.8–1.5 s / 2–4 s bands, plate and hall as the two characters
   worth shipping first, shimmer deferred.
4. **The Reese case argues for a preset**, not a feature: bass-mono crossover on,
   reverb above the crossover only. We already have the parts.

**Evidence quality, stated plainly.** Part 1 is forum and blog sentiment —
directional, not authoritative, and it reflects what trance producers say about
trance. Part 2 is design-literature and DSP description, which is stronger. The
HYPERSAW-specific hypothesis in the middle is **ours and untested**; it is the
kind of claim this project requires be labelled rather than adopted.
